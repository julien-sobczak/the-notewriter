# On Thinking

## Note: Critical Thinking

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_thinking`

> The unexamined life is not worth living.
> -- Socrates

In an era of information overload and social media echo chambers, critical thinking is more essential than ever. Don't accept information at face value—ask questions, examine evidence, consider alternative explanations. Before sharing that headline or forming an opinion, pause and think: "What evidence supports this? What might I be missing? Who benefits from this narrative?" Critical thinking is a skill that requires constant practice.

## Note: First Principles Thinking

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_principle`

> I think it's important to reason from first principles rather than by analogy.
> -- Elon Musk

Instead of accepting conventional wisdom, break problems down to their fundamental truths and rebuild from there. Ask "Why?" repeatedly until you reach bedrock facts. This approach helps you question assumptions and find innovative solutions. When everyone else is optimizing the horse-drawn carriage, first principles thinking might lead you to invent the automobile.

## Note: Systems Thinking

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_thinking`

> You can't solve problems with the same thinking that created them.
> -- Albert Einstein

Modern problems rarely have simple causes and effects. Systems thinking means understanding how parts interconnect and influence each other. That traffic jam isn't just about one slow driver—it's about road design, work schedules, public transit availability, and urban planning. When facing a problem, map out the system: identify feedback loops, unintended consequences, and leverage points where small changes create large effects.

## Note: The Beginner's Mind

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoshin`

> In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities, but in the expert's there are few.
> -- Shunryu Suzuki

Expertise can blind us to new possibilities. The beginner's mind—approaching situations with openness and curiosity, free from preconceptions—allows fresh insights. When you've been doing something the same way for years, intentionally adopt a beginner's perspective. Ask naive questions, challenge assumptions, consider what someone with zero experience might suggest. Sometimes the "obvious" solution is invisible to experts.

## Note: Thinking in Bets

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-making`

> Life is poker, not chess.
> -- Annie Duke

Most decisions involve uncertainty, yet we often treat them as right or wrong in hindsight. Better thinking acknowledges uncertainty: instead of "this will work," think "this has a 70% chance of working." This mindset helps you make better decisions under uncertainty, learn from outcomes without overfitting to results, and avoid the trap of judging decisions solely by their outcomes rather than the quality of thinking that led to them.

## Note: The Premortem

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem`

> By failing to prepare, you are preparing to fail.
> -- Benjamin Franklin

Before starting a project, imagine it has failed catastrophically. Now work backward: what went wrong? This mental exercise—called a premortem—helps identify risks you might otherwise miss. In our optimism-biased culture, explicitly imagining failure feels uncomfortable but is remarkably effective. Use it for important decisions: new job, major project, significant purchase. What could go wrong? How can you prevent it?

## Note: Inversion

`@source: https://fs.blog/inversion/`

> Invert, always invert.
> -- Carl Jacobi

Sometimes the best way to solve a problem is to flip it. Instead of asking "How do I succeed?" ask "How would I guarantee failure?" Then do the opposite. Want to be productive? List everything that would make you unproductive (poor sleep, constant distractions, no priorities), then systematically address each one. Inversion reveals hidden obstacles and often provides clearer paths forward than direct approaches.
