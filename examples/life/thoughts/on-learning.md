# On Learning

## Note: The Growth Mindset

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindset#Fixed_and_growth_mindset`

> Intelligence is not fixed, it can be developed through dedication and hard work.
> -- Carol Dweck

In the modern world of rapid technological change, the ability to learn continuously is more valuable than any single skill. A growth mindset means viewing challenges as opportunities to expand your capabilities rather than threats to your competence. When you fail at something, you're not proving you can't do it—you're discovering what you need to learn next.

## Note: The Power of Practice

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practice_(learning_method)`

> We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit.
> -- Aristotle

Modern research confirms what ancient wisdom knew: mastery comes from deliberate practice, not innate talent. Whether you're learning to code, play an instrument, or master a new language, progress comes from consistent, focused effort. Set aside time daily for practice, break complex skills into smaller components, and embrace the discomfort of being a beginner.

## Note: Learning from Failure

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Failure`

> I have not failed. I've just found 10,000 ways that won't work.
> -- Thomas Edison

In a world that celebrates success and hides failure, we forget that every expert was once a novice who kept going. Failure isn't the opposite of success—it's a necessary step toward it. When a project doesn't work out or you make a mistake, ask yourself: "What did I learn?" Document your failures as thoroughly as your successes; they're often more valuable.

## Note: The Spacing Effect

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacing_effect`

> Repetition is the mother of learning, the father of action, which makes it the architect of accomplishment.
> -- Zig Ziglar

Modern learning science shows that spacing out your study sessions is far more effective than cramming. Your brain needs time to consolidate information. Instead of studying for hours in one sitting, break your learning into shorter sessions spread over days or weeks. Use spaced repetition systems for facts and concepts you want to remember long-term.

## Note: Active Recall

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testing_effect`

> Tell me and I forget, teach me and I may remember, involve me and I learn.
> -- Benjamin Franklin

Passive reading and highlighting feel productive but are surprisingly ineffective. Active recall—forcing yourself to retrieve information from memory—strengthens neural pathways far more effectively. After reading something, close the book and try to explain it in your own words. Create flashcards, quiz yourself, teach others. Struggle is a feature of learning, not a bug.

## Note: The Feynman Technique

`@source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_by_teaching`

> If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough.
> -- Richard Feynman

In an age of information overload, the ability to distill complex ideas into simple explanations is invaluable. Try this: take a concept you're learning and explain it as if teaching a child. When you get stuck, you've found a gap in your understanding. Go back to your sources, fill the gap, then try again. This technique forces you to identify and address weaknesses in your knowledge.

## Note: Learning in Public

`@source: https://www.swyx.io/learn-in-public`

> The best way to learn is to teach.
> -- Frank Oppenheimer

Modern technology enables a powerful learning strategy: share what you're learning publicly through blogs, videos, or social media. This creates accountability, forces you to organize your thoughts, attracts feedback from others, and builds a record of your progress. Don't wait until you're an expert—document your journey from the beginning.
